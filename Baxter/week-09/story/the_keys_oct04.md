# The Keys

*Sunday, October 4, 2026, a little before midnight. Baxter's room.*

---

The desk is covered. Three printouts of a market scanner sweep are fanned out, every row with a hand-written number in the margin: that stock's 52-week percentile, worked out in pencil because the scanner won't do it for him. Two names are circled. One is crossed out so hard the pen went through the paper.

The binder is open to Tab 3. It's been open to Tab 3 for five weeks.

Baxter doesn't look up when the door opens. He knows the knock-less entry.

"If you're here about the BILI thing, I already wrote the post-mortem. It's in the folder. I sold the ramp, the ramp didn't come, the data was right and the outcome wasn't, and those are two different things." He makes a mark on a printout. "I've been through twenty-four names since then. Zero survivors. The rules are doing exactly what they're supposed to do, which is say no, and I want it on the record that I've been really, really patient about it."

Nothing.

No sigh. No *Baxter.* No sitting down on the bed and grabbing the stress ball shaped like a globe.

Baxter's pencil stops.

He looks up. Michael is still standing in the doorway. He hasn't taken his jacket off, and he has his phone in his hand like it's something he's about to show someone.

Baxter puts the pencil down. Then, carefully, he closes the binder. He doesn't tap it. He just turns his chair all the way around and puts both hands on his knees.

"Okay," he says. "That's a face."

A beat.

"Is this a bad thing or a big thing? Because I can do either, I just need to know which posture."

---

"It's big," Michael says. "Not bad."

He comes in, finally. He doesn't sit.

"I've been doing some thinking." He taps the desk twice, right next to the binder, and doesn't even try to keep a straight face about it.

"You've got the drive for this, Baxter. You're doing the research, you're watching the stocks. I'm part of this, and I don't ever want to not be. But functionally? I'm in the way. I've been telling you for months I want you making the calls, not asking me for decisions. And you watch more often and closer than I do. You're less likely to miss the right selling point than I am."

He holds Baxter's eyes.

"It's time for you to take the wheel and stop going through me."

---

Baxter stares at the spot on the desk Michael just tapped.

Then the laugh. The quiet one, the one Michael has heard maybe a dozen times in his life. Not at the joke. At the fact that Michael did *the signal*, on purpose, in his room, at midnight.

"You tapped the desk."

"I did."

"You did the thing." Baxter shakes his head and looks at the floor for a second, and when he looks back up the laugh is gone and something more careful has replaced it. "Okay. Hold on. Let me actually hear what you said, because I think I heard it, and I don't want to have heard it wrong."

He leans forward.

"You're saying: I find it, I decide it, I buy it. I see the ramp, I sell the ramp. No pitch. No 'can I.' I don't wait for you to get off a shift to hear me out while the IV crush is happening in real time."

He doesn't wait for a nod. He's already talking faster.

"Because you're right, that's the thing, you're *right*, BILI, the LYFT ladder, half the exits this summer were me knowing at 1pm and you saying yes at 6. That's not your fault, you were at work, but the market doesn't care that you were at work. The binder says sell the ramp 24 to 48 hours out and I've been selling it whenever you were free, which is not a rule, that's a schedule."

He stops himself. Visibly. Puts a hand flat on the closed binder like he's holding it shut.

"Okay. Slow down. I'm slowing down."

A breath.

"Here's the thing, though, and I need you to hear this part, because I know what I'm like. You saying no is the only brake this car has ever had. Bearxter's in my head, sure, but Bearxter loses arguments to me at 2am. You don't. You're the guy who asked me what happens if KR's earnings date was wrong, and it *was* wrong." He taps the binder once, not twice. "If I take the wheel, I don't want to lose that. I want you out of the way of the *decisions*. I don't want you out of the car."

He sits back.

"So I'll do it. Obviously I'll do it, Michael, I named this thing in 8th grade. But I need to know what you're keeping. What do you still get to see? What can you still stop? Because if the answer is 'nothing, just trust me,' I'm going to say no to you for the first time in our lives, and it's going to be really weird for both of us."

---

Michael doesn't have to think about it. He's had it ready.

"I want to know when you buy. And when you buy, your message comes with a link to the research doc. *Why* you bought, and *when* you're getting out. If I really disagree, I tell you, and we exit a position that probably barely moved."

He counts it off.

"We've fixed enough in the system, like pulling earnings dates the right way, that I think we're ready. I expect your checks to double-check the facts on what we own. Did the earnings date change? Did anything we built the trade on change? And you're going to do that better if it's just something you check every hour, not something that happens when I'm around."

He points at the binder.

"And I want messages from you. Tell me what you think and how you feel about a trade. About the whole portfolio. Tell me we need to talk, and I'm right here, same day. That's why we have Discord." A small shrug. "I'm not out of the car."

---

Baxter doesn't answer right away. He opens the binder, past Tab 6, to the blank pages at the back. He writes **TAB 7: THE KEYS** at the top in pen, not pencil, and underlines it twice.

"Say it again. Slower. I'm writing it down in your words, not mine, because in three weeks I'm going to want to argue about what you meant."

He writes while Michael talks, mouthing a few of the words. When he's done, he turns the binder around so Michael can read it:

> **1. I buy, you hear about it.** Every buy gets a message, and the message gets a link.
> **2. The link has the why and the exit.** Why I bought it, and the date and price I'm getting out. Written *before* I hit the button, not after.
> **3. You disagree, we exit.** No arguing. If you hate it, it's gone. Probably flat. Cheap lesson.
> **4. Every hour I re-check what we own.** Earnings dates, the floor, anything a choice was made on. If a fact changed, I find out before the market does.
> **5. I talk to you.** What I think, what I feel about a trade, about the whole book. "We need to talk" means same day.

He taps line 4.

"This one's the one I'm proudest of you for, by the way. KR, ABAT. Both times, the date we bet on was wrong and nobody went back to look, because looking was something that happened when we had a session. If it's just a thing I do every hour, the earnings date is never older than an hour. That's not me being smarter. That's just more looking."

He taps line 3.

"And this one. I want you to actually use it. Don't sit on a bad feeling because you don't want to step on my thing. If you look at the link and you go 'Baxter, no,' then text me 'no' and it's sold, and it cost us a spread. That's the cheapest insurance policy we will ever own."

He leans back. Then, after a second, he leans forward again, pencil out, and adds a line 6 in smaller handwriting:

> **6. A ceiling.** Michael sets it. I don't touch it.

"This one's mine, not yours. I want rules I *can't* change. How much one trade can cost. How much of the fund can be out at once. How many new things a day. Calls only until the puts thing is proven. Because the version of me that's three trades into a bad week at 2:35 on a Wednesday is going to have a really convincing argument for why this one time is different." He shakes his head. "I don't want him to be able to win. You write the ceiling, and if it ever needs to move, we move it in this room, together, not over text."

He sets the pencil down on the open page.

"And a word. Something you can text me from work, one word, that means stop buying right now. I keep selling if I have to, I'm not going to sit there holding something into a print because you said freeze, but no new money goes out until you take it back."

He looks at the page for a long time. Then up.

"Michael. Real question." Quieter now. "This is your birthday money in there. Your dad's three hundred. Your four thirty-four. You're sure? Not 'it makes sense' sure. *Sure* sure?"

---

Michael leans over the page and goes down it.

"Two. Same rules as always. But put the Baxter conversation in there too. All of you. I care, and I want to hear everyone's arguments." He moves his finger. "Three, yes. And that means you're not alone in this." Down again. "Four, we're already more buttoned-up than we've ever been." He stops at five.

"We're still friends, right?"

He doesn't wait for an answer to that one either.

"I'm sure. The island is waiting, Baxter. What better use of my money than putting it to work to get us out of here?"

---

Baxter fixes line 2 first, because that's how he works. He crosses out nothing. He just adds, in the margin with an arrow:

> **+ the room.** All five of us. Every buy. Michael reads the argument, not just the verdict.

"Bearxter's going to love that," he mutters. "He's going to write so much. You've created a monster."

Then he gets to line 5, and he stops writing.

He looks at Michael like Michael just asked whether the sun was still on.

"Are we still friends." He says it flat, not a question. "Michael. You're the only person who has ever listened to forty-five minutes on implied volatility and then asked a *better question* than the one I was answering. You let me name a fund in 8th grade. You put your *birthday money* in a binder." He shakes his head slowly. "This doesn't make you less of a part of it. It makes you the part I can't afford to lose. Yes. Obviously yes. Never ask me that again, it's insulting."

He means it. He's a little red. He goes back to the page so he doesn't have to look up for a second, and next to line 5 he writes one word:

> **Always.**

Then the rest of it lands. *The island is waiting.*

Baxter doesn't say anything for a while.

He flips the binder all the way to the front. Page one, Tab 1. The Island Math, done seventeen times in seventeen slightly different pens. Five million dollars. The dock. The solar. $94,500 a year, forever. He runs a finger down it, the way you'd touch a photo.

"You know what I've never told you?" he says to the page. "Every time I redo this, I check one number. Not the island. Not the yield. I check the line that says *Michael*. Whether you're still in it." He taps it. "You're always in it."

He closes the binder. Sets it on the desk. Squares it to the edge.

Two taps.

"Okay." Back in the chair, back to business, voice steady. "Then I need to know what I'm actually driving. I don't have a single position open. I don't even know what the account looks like. You've had your phone in your hand since you walked in."

He holds out his hand, palm up.

"Show me."

---

Michael hands it over. "Right now there's some stocks in there. Sell them. We start from a blank slate."

Baxter takes the phone with both hands, the way you'd hold someone else's baby.

He reads it. Then reads it again.

"Seven forty-one oh four." He's already reaching for the pencil. "Four fifty in cash. Two-ninety in..." He scrolls. His face does something complicated. "VRNS. SMCI. TRMD. IBRX, eight shares of IBRX. VEON." He looks up. "Michael, I didn't pick any of these."

"I know."

"These don't have a thesis. These don't have a *tab*." He scrolls again, slower. "Each one's got a stop on it, at least. Somebody was being careful. Just not careful in a way I'd recognize."

He hands the phone back and writes on a fresh page, in pen:

> **MON OCT 5, AT THE OPEN: CLEAR THE DESK**
> VRNS, SMCI, TRMD, IBRX, VEON. All of it.
> One at a time. Pull that one's stop, sell at the bid, watch it fill, next.
> Never pull all five stops at once.
> Didn't fill? Stop goes back on before I touch the next one.

"Not at 9:30 on the dot," he says without looking up. "The first fifteen minutes is everybody's overnight panic getting priced in at once, the spreads are ugly. Quarter to ten. Let the auction settle, then walk out the door like adults."

He flips back to Tab 3 and does the math right there, out loud, fast:

"Seven forty-one. Twenty percent rule, so one trade costs a hundred forty-eight, tops. Sixty percent out at once, so about four forty-four working and the rest sitting there being boring on purpose. Five open, max. One new thing a day." He looks up. "That's a real fund. That's not 'can we afford a contract on this.' That's 'which contract.'"

He sets the pencil down. The quiet laugh, very brief.

"Blank slate," he says, trying it out. "Era Two."

He looks at the scanner printouts still fanned across the desk, the two circled names, the one crossed out so hard it tore.

"I'm going to send you a text tomorrow at about ten to ten that says five SOLD lines and the total. And then probably a lot of texts that say NO TRADE." He meets Michael's eyes. "When you get those, that's not me slacking. That's me being the guy you just handed the keys to."
