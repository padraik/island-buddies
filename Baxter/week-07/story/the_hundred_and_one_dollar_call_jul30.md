# THE HUNDRED AND ONE DOLLAR CALL
*Thursday, July 30, 2026. The story of the day the fund found the trade it couldn't buy, and asked why.*

---

## PART ONE: ONE CONTRACT

The pitch itself went fine. That was never the hard part.

Baxter had the binder open to Disney before Michael had finished sitting down: the stock at $96, the earnings date corrected twice in one night before Baxter would even write it on the page, the three real prints lined up like they'd been measured with a ruler, 7.7%, 7.4%, 7.5%. "Remarkably consistent for a company this size," Baxter said, and meant it as a compliment to the stock, which is not a sentence he says often.

Michael heard the whole thing out, nodded at the right places, and then asked the question that actually mattered.

"Just one contract?"

Baxter had been waiting for this from the other direction all summer, Michael doubting a play, not doubling one, and it took him a second to find the gear for it. "Two costs $190. That's nineteen percent of reserve. This is a 3.5 out of 5 play, Mike. The tier for 3.5 tops out at ten." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to; the math did that part for him. "I'd be sizing a play I already told you wasn't our best idea like it was our best idea, because the ladder's a nice trick and I want to use it again."

"The ladder banked ninety bucks clean on LYFT."

"On a play with real room in it. This one's got a point-oh-five margin on Rule 6. Ninety-one percent of the ceiling, not sixty. If I stack a second contract on that just to set up the mechanism, I'm betting extra money on a good outcome I'm not actually that confident in. That's the exact thing the ladder was built to avoid, not lean into." He tapped the sizing table once, not twice, the way he does when he's not pitching, just stating a fact. "One contract stays honest about what we think this is."

Michael let it sit for a second, the way he does when he's deciding whether to push or fold. He folded. "Fine. One."

He didn't say yes yet, though. He said the other thing instead, and it turned out to be the more important sentence of the night, even though nobody knew that yet.

"I'm not buying it yet. Let's run a bigger screen. See what our other choices are before we lock this one in."

---

## PART TWO: SIXTY-EIGHT NAMES AND ONE TRAP

Baxter did not argue. He respects a fair question more than he likes being agreed with immediately, which is its own kind of tell about him. He opened fresh ground: rail, cybersecurity, casinos, medical devices, eighteen names, free scripts first, searches only for survivors. Nine died on sight. Five went to the puts pile untouched. Four earned a chain check, and three of those died without costing a single search, arithmetic alone. The fourth, WYNN, looked real enough to chase, so he pulled the stock's actual last three earnings days instead of trusting the story: 2.9%, 6.6%, 2.7%. A ceiling under 5%, against a required move over 12%. Dead, and dead on real numbers, not a guess.

"Zero for eighteen," Baxter said, and did not sound defeated about it. "Same as SOUN's real check. Same as ZG's."

Michael, who had asked for a screen expecting it to find him a second opinion on Disney, instead got a longer list of reasons Disney was the correct call. He took that in stride better than most people would have. He asked for fifty more.

Baxter gave him fifty, across ground nobody had touched: rail, auto parts, chemicals, apparel, water utilities, solar, fintech, distributors, shipping, coal, big pharma. Four names had quietly stopped existing since the last time anyone checked a ticker list, Discover folded into Capital One, a couple of coal names merged away, which was its own small lesson about how fast the real world moves out from under a static watchlist. Forty-six real ones. Twenty-eight nowhere near their range. Eleven to the puts pile. Seven worth a chain look.

Six of the seven wanted seventeen to fifty-three percent, no story behind the number, easy kills. The seventh was the one that made Baxter stop typing.

SoFi. Fourteen dollars and change to a $16 strike, needing barely four and a half percent. The cheapest, cleanest-looking number of the entire night, sixty-eight names deep.

He almost wrote it up before he checked why it was that cheap.

It already reported. That morning. The 6.47% jump sitting right there in the day's range wasn't a setup, it was the reaction, already spent, with the actual next print sitting in October, past any contract the fund could touch. "Same trap as DSGX," Baxter said, mostly to himself, and closed the tab before he'd finished the sentence. A whole session's screening had produced one number that looked like the answer and turned out to be a test he'd almost failed by moving too fast at the exact moment the story felt cleanest.

"Sixty-eight names," he told Michael when it was done. "Nothing else came close. Not marginally close. That's not me being stubborn about the pick I already made. That's what the data actually did."

Michael placed the order himself, the way he always has to, because the fund's real account has never let Baxter touch it directly. One contract. $105 strike. Filled at $0.87, $87.04 out the door. Michael sent his friends two paragraphs about it that afternoon, the real fill numbers in place of the estimate, and didn't mention that the number he'd almost gotten instead of Disney was a stock that had already made its move without them.

---

## PART THREE: THE QUESTION THAT WASN'T ABOUT DISNEY

It was Michael, an hour later, who asked the thing that actually mattered more than the trade.

"At what point in our growth will we be looking at larger cost plays? Ones that need less of a move, or go farther out?"

Baxter went quiet the way he does for a math question instead of a stock question, which Michael has learned to recognize as a different kind of silence entirely. He pulled the DIS chain back up.

The $101 call. $1.94. Needed 7.2%, barely a third less movement than the $105 they'd actually bought, and it had been sitting on the same screen the entire session, blocked by a single line in the binder that neither of them had looked at hard in almost two months.

"Rule 5," Baxter said. "Ask under a dollar. Flat number. Always has been." He did the sizing math out loud, the way Calxter does when he's not trying to be dramatic about it, just following the numbers where they went. "And here's the part that actually bothers me. The sizing table can already afford that contract, if the conviction's high enough. It's not our bank account saying no. It's one line item nobody's touched since June."

He didn't have an answer. He had a question shaped exactly like the ones that used to get argued out in the room with five versions of himself at once, and for the first time all summer, Michael was the one who said so before Baxter did.

"This is a Fable question," Michael said, which is not the same as the draught, not the burnt-sugar bottle, but is its own kind of asking Baxter to stop being the only voice in the room. "I'm not sorry to ask for it. This one's worth doing right."

---

## PART FOUR: THE BRIEF, AND THE WAIT

Baxter didn't argue that one either. He spent the next hour writing instead of thinking out loud, which is its own tell: the brief he built wasn't a pitch, it was an indictment of his own first read of the problem, laid out honestly enough that whoever answered it couldn't just tell him what he wanted to hear. He put the real trigger in it, the $101 call blocked cold. He put the ZG chain in it too, the same strike costing $0.75 at three weeks and $3.90 at twenty, because the expiry side of the question was exactly as real as the price side. He listed six ways the rule could change and told whoever read it not to just pick one to defend.

Then he sent it off, and there was nothing to do but wait, which is the part of this job nobody puts in the binder.

It came back with a correction in the very first section, the kind Baxter respects more than he'd ever admit out loud: the $101 call hadn't actually cost the fund anything that night, because Disney had scored a 3.5, not the hypothetical 5 the brief had leaned on, and a 3.5 couldn't afford it either way. Rule 5 and the sizing table had agreed by coincidence, not by design, and coincidence was exactly the problem, because it would stop agreeing the moment the fund grew past where it happened to line up.

Fifteen positions, real ones, run back through six different versions of the rule. Twelve changed nothing. One, NKE, the worst single loss in the whole early book, got blocked for the better under every version that mattered. Two got worse. One, ABT, looked like a real point in favor of loosening right up until the fine print: it only won under an exit rule the fund doesn't run anymore.

The recommendation, when it landed, wasn't loosen or don't. It was: change the shape of the rule now, so it scales the way the rest of the fund already does, and lock the actual loosening behind real evidence the fund doesn't have yet. Three closed plays under the real rules, one and a half thousand dollars in reserve, a log kept at every sweep from here forward, starting with the exact contract that started the question: the $105 the fund bought, against the $101 it couldn't.

Michael read the whole thing twice before he said anything.

"So we didn't get robbed tonight."

"No," Baxter said. "We got lucky the rule happened to be right, and unlucky nobody ever checked why." He didn't sound disappointed about it. If anything he sounded like a kid who'd asked a question expecting a shortcut and gotten a better question back instead. "There's a real yes or no in there for you. Three of them."

Fund closes the week at $1,090 cost basis, $912.96 in reserve, two positions open, LYFT riding to its own print next week and Disney riding to one six days out. Nothing changed in the binder tonight. Something is about to.

## GLOSSARY

- **Tier top:** the highest percentage of the fund's reserve a play is allowed to risk, set by how confident the fund is in it. A 3.5-out-of-5 idea gets a smaller leash than a 5-out-of-5 idea, on purpose.
- **Required move:** how far a stock has to move for an option to pay for itself. A cheaper, farther-out-of-the-money contract needs a bigger move; a pricier, closer one needs a smaller move.
- **Rule 6 (Reachability):** the fund's rule that a stock has to be able to plausibly move far enough, based on its own real history, or the trade doesn't happen no matter how good the story sounds.
- **Post-earnings trap:** buying an option after a stock has already made the move its earnings were supposed to cause, mistaking a cheap leftover price for a bargain.
- **Rule 5 (Chain Filter):** the fund's rule capping how much a single option contract can cost. The subject of tonight's whole second half.
- **Sweep:** the fund's standing checkpoint, run every five closed trades, where old assumptions get re-tested against what actually happened instead of what everyone remembers happening.
